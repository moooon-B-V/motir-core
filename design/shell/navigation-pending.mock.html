The navigation-pending grammar

What the authed content area shows between the click and the arrival: the THREE windows of the wait, the shell’s mark on the first, the page’s own in-page frame on the second, the 120 ms delay that stops a fast route flashing either, which surfaces earn a frame, and the rule that a view switch needing no server shows no pending state at all. MOTIR-3431 drew it; MOTIR-3492 re-mounted it after a route-group boundary was found to destroy the 404 on all 11 authed routes that decide existence. MOTIR-3433 and MOTIR-3435 were reverted; MOTIR-3434 shipped and is untouched.

Panel A · The transition, drawn — a HARD navigation the shell is the same object in all four frames; only <main>'s child changes. Window 1 is structurally EMPTY here, and Panel E is the soft navigation, where it is not

A reader on Work Items types /items for another project into the address bar — the second symptom this story was reported for. The top bar, the rail and the context path belong to app/(authed)/layout.tsx and are unchanged by a navigation inside the group; drawing them four times identical is the claim, not decoration. The region that changes is <main>'s single child, and it is the only thing this design owns.

A TYPED URL is a document request, so there is no mounted shell to speak for window 1. Nothing can be shown between the address being entered and the page's own gate resolving — at any cost, by any instrument. That is not a gap in this design: it is why shrinking the gate is the substantive work and the frame is the visible half. Panel D carries the three windows; Panel E carries what a reader who CLICKED gets instead.

Panel B · The frame, and what each block stands in for — the no-shift claim, block by block

A layout shift on settle is VERTICAL. The frame and the page both fill <main>'s width, so a placeholder bar being a different WIDTH from the title it stands in for moves nothing — a block-level line box occupies its whole line either way. What moves the page is a block whose height, or the gap above it, differs from the region it is replaced by. So the frame matches heights and gaps exactly, and takes its widths from what reads as a page rather than from any particular one.

The subtitle block is the one honest compromise, and it is stated rather than hidden. Drawing it is the better half: an upward settle pulls content toward the reader’s eye rather than away from it, and the pages that carry no subtitle (Work Items, Boards) are also the fastest ones, which under the reveal delay never show the frame at all. A route that both carries no subtitle AND reads slowly passes its OWN body to the shared primitive rather than taking this generic one — which under this revision is a prop, not a second boundary. Panel C · The reveal delay — 120 ms, and the argument for it

An affordance that paints the instant its window opens makes a fast one worse: the surface is replaced with a grey frame, holds it for 80 ms, and is replaced again with content — two paints where the reader asked for one, read as a flicker rather than as speed. So every pending state here is mounted immediately and revealed on a delay. Mounted, because the reader must never be left looking at something that is no longer true; revealed late, because a frame nobody needed is worse than no frame.

ONE declaration, referenced by BOTH windows. The shell’s mark (window 1, Panel E) and the page’s frame (window 2) share this keyframe and this delay, and each window’s clock starts when that window opens: the mark’s at the click, the frame’s at the first flush. Two pending states revealing on two timings is the flicker this delay exists to remove, wearing a second costume — which is why the declaration is referenced and never re-declared.

In under 120 ms
a cached segment, an empty settings pane 		The previous surface, then the destination. Nothing pending is ever visible. One paint. 0.1 s is the classical ceiling for a response that reads as instantaneous. Inside it the navigation has already succeeded, and a state placed there is an interruption the reader has to parse and discard. 120 ms is that ceiling plus one 60 Hz frame of slack, so a route that lands one frame late is still treated as instant.
In longer than 120 ms
Work Items, the work-item page, anything with a real read 		The mark, then the frame, fading in before their eye has finished travelling to the content region. The navigation has ALREADY failed to feel instantaneous, so a pending state is not an interruption — it is the only evidence the click landed. A saccade to the content region lands around 150–200 ms after the click, so a reveal begun at 120 ms is in place before the reader looks at it, and they never see it arrive.

It is CSS, not a timer. animation: nav-pending-reveal 90ms ease-out 120ms both on the frame, with the keyframe going opacity: 0 → 1. No useEffect, no setTimeout, no client state, nothing to clean up when the navigation is abandoned — the element is removed and the animation goes with it. A route that resolves in 40 ms unmounts the frame 80 ms before it would have become visible, and no code had to decide that.

Under prefers-reduced-motion: reduce the delay stays and the motion goes. The reveal becomes a step at 120 ms with no fade, and the pulse stops — the frame is static blocks. The delay is not an animation preference: it is what stops a fast route showing a state it does not need, and a reader who has asked for less motion has asked for fewer flashes, not more.

Panel D · Where the frame MOUNTS — three windows, and the one of them no boundary may cover

A loading.tsx fallback renders as soon as its ancestor layouts resolve, which is BEFORE the page function runs. That flushes the response head and fixes the HTTP status at 200, so a notFound() reached later renders the not-found BODY under a 200 and the 404 is gone. 11 of the 58 app/(authed) pages call notFound() — three of those assertions are tenant-isolation contracts, and /items/[key]'s own source calls its 404 a no existence leak guarantee. A group boundary breaks all 11 at once. Hoisting the gate into a layout.tsx above the page does not help and was BUILT AND MEASURED: a layout is an ANCESTOR of the boundary, so resolving it is precisely what RELEASES the fallback.

The frame was never the error. Giving windows 1 and 2 the same boundary was.

Panel E · The shell’s MARK — window 1, and only on a SOFT navigation. Drawn at rest and marked

On a soft navigation the shell is already mounted, so a client affordance renders with no server boundary anywhere — and therefore cannot touch a status. On a document request it does not render at all: the shell is being produced by the server for the first time and there is no pending navigation to have. That asymmetry is the whole reason this instrument is safe where a loading.tsx is not.

It is a MARK on the thing the reader clicked, not a skeleton. The shell does not know the destination's shape. A skeleton drawn by the shell would be a guess that the page's own frame then replaces — two frames for one navigation, which is the flicker the reveal delay exists to remove, wearing a third costume.

Why the ACTIVE treatment and not the hover one. The hover fill was the first answer and it is wrong for one specific reason: the reader's pointer is usually still on the item they just clicked, so a held hover tint is indistinguishable from the state they already had — a mark that only appears when nobody is looking at it. The active treatment is unambiguous against hover, invents no token, and is semantically what is happening: the rail says you are going there rather than something is loading. If the navigation is abandoned the mark unwinds with useLinkStatus and the rail is as it was.

One board artefact. The marked sample forces the reveal's end state, because a board is a still. Everything in both strips is the shipped component's own markup and its own classes — the mark is a state the rail item already HAS, moved in time, not a treatment this board invented.

Panel F · The locator cost — decided, and the decision is not to incur it

The cost belongs to the ROUTE boundary, not to the frame. A route-level fallback makes React retain the previous route's subtree mounted-and-hidden while the new one streams, so both are in the DOM at once. Playwright resolves locators before filtering on visibility, so an unscoped getByText / getByTestId / getByLabel matches BOTH and fails strict mode. One group boundary turned 30 assertions across 17 spec files red at once, and exactly zero of the 30 used getByRole, which is immune because the accessibility tree excludes the hidden copy.

An in-page <Suspense> renders INSIDE the destination page, so the previous route is already gone and there is one subtree in the DOM. Measured on the same spec file rather than argued:

tests/e2e/issue-detail-flow.spec.ts result
with the (authed) group boundary 8 of 16 failed
the boundary removed, nothing else changed 16 passed
with the two IN-PAGE boundaries /items/[key] ships today 16 passed

— and that file still contains 23 unscoped page.getByText / getByTestId / getByLabel calls against 76 getByRole ones, counted at origin/main 4fd55464. It is the spec most exposed to the collision, and it is green.

So: scope the BOUNDARIES, not the assertions. No spec is rewritten by this design and none needs to be; scoping the 30 to getByRole stays good practice on its own merits and is a prerequisite of nothing here. The standing rule this leaves: a route-level boundary is a SUITE-WIDE change and an in-page one is a LOCAL change. If a route boundary is ever reintroduced, the 30 come back with it — and that is the number to weigh at the moment of reintroducing it, not afterwards.

Panel G · The switch rule — a view switch that needs no server shows NO pending state at all

When a toggle’s target body needs no new server data, there is nothing to wait for. Draw no spinner, disable no segment, show no skeleton, dim nothing. The body is simply there, in the same frame as the click. A pending affordance on such a switch is a defect, not a courtesy: it manufactures a wait the reader would not otherwise have had, and it is the exact complaint this story was reported for, reintroduced as a feature.
