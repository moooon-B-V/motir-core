Sign-in — the two-factor challenge

Story 8.11 (MOTIR-1213) · MOTIR-1216. The step between the password and the session: a six-digit code, two fallbacks, and the opt-in that stops Motir asking on this browser for 30 days. It is the THIRD step of the shipped SignInCard, in the shipped app/(auth)/layout.tsx frame — not a new page and not a new route. Six panels: where it sits · at rest · fallbacks · a recovery code · failures · dark parity. The account pane that sets all this up is design/settings/two-factor.mock.html.

PANEL 1 · Where this screen comes from. The access-path gate asks the design to draw the door, and this screen's door is not a nav item — it is a RESPONSE. `signIn.email` either returns a session (steps 1→2→4, exactly what ships today) or answers { twoFactorRedirect: true }, and step 3 appears in the same card. Two consequences the build should not have to infer: there is no route to link to and no way to reach this screen deliberately, and a reader with a trusted device SKIPS it — the flow is 1→2→4 again, which is the whole point of the checkbox on step 3.
Step 1 Email Google button, email field, Continue. SignInCard · step 'email'
Step 2 Password Email read-only, password, Continue. SignInCard · step 'password'
Step 3 · new Second factor Only when the sign-in answers twoFactorRedirect. Skipped entirely on a trusted device. SignInCard · step 'twoFactor'
Step 4 Landed The post-auth destination — ?next=, else /home. resolvePostAuthDestination
PANEL 2 · The authenticator challenge. The primary method, asked for first, in the shipped (auth) card. Three things are decided here. The headline names the ACCOUNT, not the mechanism — a reader who has just typed a password wants to know they are still signing in as themselves. The trust checkbox is UNCHECKED by default and states the thirty days rather than implying them, because it is a security decision the reader is making on their own behalf. And "Try another way" is a link at the foot, not three buttons in the body: the fallbacks are the exception, and drawing all three up front makes the strongest method look like one option among equals.

PANEL 3 · Try another way, and the emailed code. The list is ORDERED by strength and each row says what it costs, so a reader choosing under pressure is not choosing blind. Email keeps the same "lower security" language the settings pane uses — one caveat, worded once, wherever the method appears. The sent state is the one that has to be honest: it names the address, states the three-minute life, and offers a resend that is disabled until a countdown elapses, because a resend button that always works is a resend button people press four times.

PANEL 4 · A recovery code, and the last one. A different field from the six-digit boxes — a recovery code is xxxxx-xxxxx, so it gets one mono input, not six cells that would refuse the hyphen. The second card is the state worth designing rather than discovering: spending the LAST code leaves an account with 2FA on and no way back in if the authenticator is also gone. Motir says so BEFORE the reader spends it, and lands them on the settings pane afterwards rather than dropping them at /home with the problem still true.

PANEL 5 · Wrong, expired, and spent. Three refusals with three different remedies, so three different messages — a single "that didn't work" would leave a reader with a correctly-typed code and a phone whose clock has drifted with nothing to try. The attempts message is the one that must not lie by omission: the plugin allows five, and the screen counts down out loud rather than failing silently on the sixth. All three keep the reader on the step they are on; none bounces back to the password.
